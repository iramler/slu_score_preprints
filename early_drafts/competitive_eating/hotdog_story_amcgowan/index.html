Introduction

Nathan’s Famous, an American hot dog brand established in Brooklyn, New York, in 1916, is widely recognized for its annual hot dog eating contest held each year on July 4th. Originally created as a promotional event to increase brand visibility, the contest has evolved into what many call the “Super Bowl” of competitive eating. Beginning in 2008, the event was standardized at a duration of ten minutes, though earlier competitions ranged from 2.5 to 12 minutes long. In order to compete, participants must be at least 18 years old and qualify by winning a smaller regional contest. On the day of the event, between 15 and 30 competitive eaters participate. In 2011, the organizers introduced separate divisions for men and women, resulting in two first-place winners each year. The top five competitors in each division receive cash prizes, with first place earning $10,000 in 2025.

In this module, you will learn how to use the quarto extension closeread to tell a story using data from Nathan’s Hot Dog Contest. Upon completion of this module, you will have a Closeread document that looks similar to this: Example Closeread

::: {.callout-note collapse = “true” title = “Learning Objectives” appearance = “minimal”} By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  1. Identify meaningful questions about a dataset.

  2. Transform a dataset to answer those questions.

  3. Use the Closeread Quarto extension to create a visually engaging story. :::

::: {.callout-note collapse = “true” title = “Activity Length” appearance = “minimal”} This could be suitable of an out of class activity or one that spans one or more class periods. :::

::: {.callout-note collapse = “true” title = “Methods” appearance = “minimal”} Technology Requirement:

This activity is designed to be completed primarily in R using a Quarto document workflow with a Closeread format. Students should be comfortable importing data and making basic plots with packages from the tidyverse (especially readr, dplyr, and ggplot2).

No previous experience with Closeread is required. :::

Data

The HotdogData.csv dataset contains 69 rows, each representing a winner of Nathan’s Hot Dog Eating Contest. Returning winners appear multiple times, and many years include two winners (one man and one woman). The primary variables in the dataset are Dogs and Time.

Download Data: HotdogData.csv

Variable Descriptions
Variable Description
Year Year the competition took place
Sex Sex of the winner (0 = Woman, 1 = Man)
Dogs Total hot dogs consumed by the winner
Winner First and Last Name of the Winner
Time Duration of the competition in minutes

Installing Closeread

Follow along below or refer to the Closeread homepage for more information about installation.

  1. In RStudio, open the project folder where you’d like to work.

  2. At the bottom of the RStudio window, locate the Console. Next to it, select the Terminal tab.

  3. In the terminal, paste and run the following command:

quarto add qmd-lab/closeread

  1. When prompted with messages like “Do you trust the authors of this extension?” or “Would you like to continue?”, type Y into the terminal to confirm and proceed with the installation.

  2. After completing these steps, the Quarto extension Closeread will be installed.

Understanding Closeread

Before we get started on a Quarto document, please take a moment to read through this section to learn more about how to use Closeread.

To use the Closeread extension in a Quarto document, you have to include the following in your document header:

---
format:
  closeread-html:
---

You can customize your Closeread with cr-style, which you include in your header.

Click to reveal some basic customization options 
---
format:
  closeread-html: 
    cr-style:
  
      # Sidebar Background (text area as you scroll)
      narrative-background-color-sidebar: darkgreen
      
      # Section Background (area next to the sidebar)
      section-background-color: white
      
      # Sidebar Text Color
      narrative-text-color-sidebar: white
---

If you want to customize your Closeread further, there are more options here: Closeread Styling Page

To make a Closeread document, you need a section, a sticky, and a trigger.

::: {.callout-note collapse = “true” title = “Section” appearance = “minimal”} A section (cr-section) enables Closeread features. If you type inside of the section, the text will appear on your sidebar. If you type outside of your section, the text will render like it would on a normal Quarto document. This is how you format a section:


Anything you put here would render normally 

:::{.cr-section} 

You can put graphs, text, and pictures here. 

When you are typing the text for your sidebars, this is where you put it.

This is also where you will put your stickies!

:::

:::

::: {.callout-note collapse = “true” title = “Sticky” appearance = “minimal”} Stickies (e.g. #cr-ID) stay stuck to the page, fixed in place while your sidebar text changes. You can name the ID part of the label anything you’d like, for example #cr-HotdogPlot or #cr-table. This is how you format a sticky:


:::{.cr-section} 

:::{#cr-ID}

# You can put a code chunk with a plot here

:::

:::

:::

::: {.callout-note collapse = “true” title = “Trigger” appearance = “minimal”} A trigger (e.g. @cr-ID) allows you to make something from the section, like text, be the signal that a particular graph should appear on the right of the sidebar at the same time as whatever text the trigger is on. This is how you format a trigger:

:::{.cr-section}

:::{#cr-myplot}

# You would put the code chunk for my plot in here

:::

This text would appear at the same time as my plot. @cr-myplot

:::

:::

If you would like more information about the three parts of a Closeread (the section, stickies, and triggers) you can review this page: Closeread Components Guide

Now we know how Kobayashi did it, but how much faster is his method, compared to earlier years? In our plot, the earliest winners ate a lot fewer hotdogs than more recent champions. Maybe what early winners lacked in the amount they were able to eat, they made up for in speed?

How can we look at how fast winners were eating hotdogs, not just the total amount of hotdogs they were able to eat?

Let’s make a new variable to help us see how the speed of winners has changed over time.

::: {.callout-note title = “Exercise 8: Context Matters” icon = false} a. Why do you think early winners have such impressive hotdogs per minute rates?

Click for answer

The contest has not always been set to 10 minutes long. Early competitions were between 2 to 5 minutes long, eaters are able to go as fast as possible the whole time.

Longer races, like all of the ones Kobayashi competed in, were 10-12 minutes meaning that eaters had more time to get tired and slow down, causing their rates to look similar to earlier competitors who were not using the Solomon Method.

Some important context to remember about the competition is that the duration has 
varied over the years. In this plot, brighter colors (e.g. yellow) show years 
where the contest was longer and darker colors (e.g. blue) show years when the 
contest was shorter. @cr-plot3

Competitors who won when the contest was only 2 to 5 minutes long were able to eat 
at full speed the entire time. Later winners, like Kobayashi, were eating for much 
longer, leaving time for them to slow down from fatigue. Knowing this, we can 
reason that early winners were not able to eat as many total dogs, despite matching
Kobayashi's pace, because they did not have as much time to eat nor the technique 
to continue at that pace for a longer duration.

No matter the duration, it definitely takes dedication and a strong stomach to 
place first in this famous competitive eating contest.
